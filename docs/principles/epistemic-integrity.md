# Epistemic Integrity

**Project:** E-Cropolis · Project Lichen  
**Status:** Foundational principle — version 0.1  
**Established:** 2026-10-11

> Don't simulate the answer. Simulate the relationships from which answers can emerge.

## Purpose

Epistemic integrity is the discipline of preserving the distinction between what we observe, what we infer, what we assume, what we value, and what we do not yet know.

E-Cropolis explores living systems, settlements, resource flows, governance and distributed intelligence. Its simulations must make their assumptions inspectable rather than smuggling desired outcomes into their rules.

Epistemic integrity is a practice, not a claim of infallibility.

## Commitments

1. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Label measured observations, sourced facts, model assumptions, hypotheses, fictional elements, value judgments and speculative scenarios distinctly.
2. **Preserve provenance.** Record the origin, date, geographic and temporal scope, units, methods and limitations of consequential data.
3. **Make uncertainty visible.** Prefer ranges, confidence qualifications and sensitivity tests to unjustified precision. Unknown is an acceptable result.
4. **Enable reproducibility.** Record code revision, parameters, random seeds, datasets, transformations, environment and outputs sufficiently to repeat experiments.
5. **Invite falsification.** State what observations would weaken or overturn a claim. Seek counterexamples and compare competing explanations.
6. **Distinguish the model from the world.** A convincing simulation is not proof of ecological or social validity. Validate against independent observations where possible.
7. **Expose normative choices.** Define who benefits, who bears costs, which capabilities matter and why. No metric is ethically neutral merely because it is numerical.
8. **Correct the record.** Preserve meaningful revisions and acknowledge errors, failed experiments and changed conclusions.
9. **Apply the same scrutiny to AI outputs.** Generated claims, code and analyses require verification appropriate to their consequences.
10. **Respect living systems and people.** Consider ecological limits, distributional effects, consent, privacy and long-term stewardship rather than optimizing a single proxy.

## Value is not price

Financial price, revenue, collateral value, novelty, useful capability and regenerative value are different concepts.

A functioning device may lose resale value while remaining useful. Conversely, expensive new infrastructure may generate revenue without delivering proportionate ecological or social benefit. Neither conclusion should be presumed: both require evidence.

**Working principle:** The useful life of technology should be evaluated by the capabilities it continues to provide, not merely by the availability of its successor.

Replacement decisions should compare continued use, repair, repurposing, sharing and new procurement. Account for operational energy, embodied energy and materials, water, maintenance, performance, reliability, rebound effects, and end-of-life impacts over a stated time horizon.

## A provisional value heuristic

For discussion, not as a validated physical or economic law:

```text
Regenerative value heuristic =
    (Useful capability × Epistemic integrity) / Resources consumed
```

The expression is a **conceptual guide**, not yet a calibrated index. Before quantifying it, define each term, its units, boundaries, weights and uncertainty. Multiplying unlike or subjective measures without a defensible measurement model risks false precision. A ratio must not conceal thresholds, irreversibility, equity or ecological harms. Report component measures alongside any composite score.

An avoided expenditure or avoided resource use may represent real value even when it produces no new sale. The counterfactual must be explicit and tested.

## Data Distance and computational friction

E-Cropolis should investigate whether useful computation can be delivered with less unnecessary movement, transformation and replacement of resources.

Potential dimensions include:

- **Physical/network distance:** Where data travels and why.
- **Computational distance:** Work and memory movement required before a useful result emerges.
- **Representational distance:** Transformations between a phenomenon and its computable representation, including what is lost or gained.
- **Epistemic distance:** How far a conclusion is from inspectable supporting evidence.
- **Capability distance:** The gap between available technology and capabilities actually accessible to people and communities.

Hypotheses about local inference, smaller models, compute-in-memory, caching and reuse must be benchmarked against realistic alternatives. Efficiency improvements may also increase total demand; measure system-wide outcomes rather than assuming savings.

## Requirements for E-Cropolis experiments

Each substantive experiment should document:

| Field | Required record |
| --- | --- |
| Question | The claim or relationship being tested |
| Status | Observation, hypothesis, assumption, fiction or normative choice |
| Sources | Provenance, date, license where relevant, and known limitations |
| Boundary | Geography, timescale, population and system exclusions |
| Method | Code version, data transformations, parameters and random seeds |
| Measures | Units, baselines, uncertainties and relevant resource costs |
| Comparison | Counterfactual or alternative explanation |
| Results | Observations separate from interpretations |
| Challenges | Failure modes, sensitivity and disconfirming evidence |
| Revision | What changed, why, and what remains unresolved |

For the fictional 650-acre E-Cropolis site near Monroe, Oregon, fictional ownership and parcel boundaries must remain explicitly separate from empirical watershed, soil, climate and public-land data.

## Decision rule

Before presenting a finding or recommending a change, ask:

1. What do we actually know, and how do we know it?
2. Which assumptions or missing observations could change the conclusion?
3. What useful capabilities remain, expand or disappear?
4. What resources are consumed, displaced or preserved, and for whom?
5. What would cause us to revise our position?

## Open questions

- Can useful capability be measured without reducing living systems to simplistic proxies?
- When does keeping older hardware create more total value than replacing it?
- How should a simulation represent uncertainty in ecological feedbacks and governance?
- Can reduced computational friction be measured as value creation even without increased revenue?
- How can communities audit the evidence and normative choices embedded in E-Cropolis?
